The problem

Stars measure popularity, not health, and the real risk sits where nobody is looking

2016

left-pad

One maintainer unpublished an 11-line npm package, and builds broke across the industry.

2021

Log4Shell

A critical flaw in a logging library that many teams did not know they used, because it arrived transitively.

2024

xz backdoor

An attacker spent years gaining the trust of the overstretched maintainer of a library used across Linux.

Each incident was a dependency-chain or maintainer-concentration problem. Tools that score one repository at a time cannot see either.

The idea

Faultline tracks health, maps hidden risk, recommends what to use, and tests itself

Track

Is this package healthy?

Eight activity and risk signals become one transparent score, with trends and early decline flags.

Map

Where does risk hide?

A knowledge graph shows transitive risk, maintainer concentration, blast radius and licence conflicts.

Recommend

What should I use?

Describe a need and get a ranked pick, with every reason traced to the score.

Prove

Does the score work?

A published backtest against stars, commit recency and OpenSSF Scorecard.

Version 1: about 2,000 PyPI packages, refreshed about every 30 minutes, planned at $0 running cost on free tiers.

The score

One transparent, versioned formula that anyone can recompute and challenge

Maintainer activity20%
Contributor retention15%
Responsiveness15%
Dependency risk (graph)15%
PR flow10%
Release recency10%
Bus factor10%
Open vulnerabilities5%

Percentile-based. Each signal becomes a 0 to 100 percentile within the tracked set, then combines by weight.

Not circular. Dependency risk uses each dependency's own health, without its dependency term.

Versioned. Every score row stores its formula version, so results can be reproduced.

Disciplined. Weights are starting values, tuned only on the backtest's training window.

Start weights for formula v1; they sum to 100%.

Describe a need, and get a ranked pick with reasons that trace back to the data

Explanations use templates, not a language model, so they are free, testable and cannot invent a fact.

Design choices

Every choice is deliberate, free today, and has a named path to larger scale

LayerChoiceWhyAt larger scale
OrchestrationGitHub Actions cronNo server to runAirflow or Dagster
ProcessingDuckDB + dbtFiltered data fits on one machineWarehouse or Spark
LakeParquet on Hugging FacePermanent, columnar, versionedObject storage + Iceberg
GraphNeo4j AuraDB FreeReadable multi-hop path queriesPaid Neo4j or Memgraph
Searchpgvector on NeonVectors and filters in one queryDedicated vector database
ServingFastAPI, EvidenceTyped API; static site survives failuresCloud Run, Superset

Why batch, not Kafka? Package health moves over days and weeks, so a 30-minute refresh supports the same decisions with no always-on server.

Safe re-runs. The daily GH Archive run overwrites the 30-minute numbers by date and hour, so data is corrected and never double-counted.

Risks and responsibility

The main risks are known, and each one already has a mitigation in the plan

RiskMitigation
GitHub rate limits: about 4,000 calls an hour against a 5,000 limitETags, GraphQL batching, or a 30-minute refresh for only the most active repositories
Incomplete PyPI-to-GitHub mappingPyPI and deps.dev links, a manual override file, and a public "unmapped" report
Neo4j free-tier cap or idle pauseStay under 80% of the cap; rebuild from the lake; Postgres as fallback
Late or disabled scheduled jobsShow last-updated time and alert on staleness
A wrong or gamed scoreOpen components, an "insufficient data" state, published misses
Scope creepShip the MVP, then the graph, before anything new

Maintainers are people. Concentration is shown as a property of a package, never a ranking of people, and public wording is "needs support", not "dying".

Speaker notes

What to say on each slide (about 12 minutes in total)

1. Title (20 sec)Say the name, then the one-line pitch. State up front that this is a plan and the build starts now.
2. The problem (1 min)Open with: "How do you decide a library is safe? Most of us check the stars." Walk through the three incidents quickly. The point is the pattern, not the details.
3. The need (40 sec)Pick the group closest to your audience and speak to it directly. Mention regulation only as a tailwind.
4. The gap (1 min)Be fair to these tools, since they are good at what they do. Say "in our survey" when making the claim, and note the features will be re-checked.
5. The idea (40 sec)Four verbs: track, map, recommend, prove. Stress that the fourth is the unusual one.
6. Core insight (1 min 15 sec)This is the key slide, so slow down. Follow the chain left to right, land on Package D, and explain why risk 32 comes from three levels down. Make clear the example is invented.
7. The score (45 sec)Do not read the bars. Point to the red one, say it is what others lack, then give the "versioned" line.
8. Recommendation (45 sec)Read the example need aloud. Explain that templates were chosen over an LLM because an explanation must not invent a reason.
9. Architecture (1 min 15 sec)Read it top down. Three ideas: one lake as the truth, one copy per access pattern, and visitors only read copies.
10. Design choices (45 sec)Show the thinking, not the logos. Pick one row, then the Kafka answer.
11. The proof (1 min 15 sec)Say plainly that there are no results yet. Explain "tune early, report late", and why the definition is committed before looking at data.
12. Roadmap (45 sec)Gates matter more than dates. Each stage has a pass test. Point out that the MVP comes first, then the differentiators.
13. Demo points (45 sec)Describe the story you will show. Do not imply any of it runs today.
14. Risks (45 sec)Name the rate-limit risk first, because it is the most concrete. Close on the maintainers line, since it shows judgment.
15. Summary (30 sec)Repeat the opening line about stars, give the three numbers as plans, and name Stage 0 as the next step.

Speaker notes

Likely questions and short answers

QuestionAnswer
Why three stores for 2,000 packages?Access patterns need it, not scale. The cost is more moving parts, contained because the other stores are rebuildable copies of the lake.
Why not stream with Kafka?Health moves over days and weeks. A 30-minute batch supports the same decisions with no always-on server.
How do you handle late or duplicate data?Writes replace a date-hour partition, and the daily run overwrites the 30-minute numbers. A retry replaces and never appends.
How do you know the data is right?dbt tests on keys, nulls, values, row counts and freshness. A failed test stops the publish step.
How do you map a package to its repo?PyPI project URLs and deps.dev links, plus a manual override file. Unmapped packages are reported, not dropped.
Why DuckDB and not Spark?After early filtering the data fits on one machine, and the dbt SQL can move to a warehouse later.
How does this scale?Swap Actions for Airflow, the dataset for Iceberg, DuckDB for a warehouse, and Neo4j Free for paid. The layers stay the same.
Why templates and not an LLM?They are deterministic, testable and free, and every sentence traces to a score component.
What if the API sleeps or Neo4j pauses?The static dashboard is unaffected, the API cold-starts, and pipeline writes keep Neo4j active. The graph can be rebuilt from the lake.
What if the backtest fails?Then we publish that. A clear negative result is still useful, and the graph comparison shows what to change.
